The number of papers published per year is, in practice, the primary currency of academic research. It is the metric by which grants are awarded, positions are secured, and careers are made.

There have always been those who produced few but foundational and brilliant works, and those who sustained careers on a steady accumulation of more applied and technical contributions. As our understanding of the universe has deepened, so too naturally have the difficulty of the problems. A byproduct is that the incentives for confronting problems with high-risk, novel, and unconventional ideas has shifted towards more predicable and incremental approaches. Until very recently, the gap between foundational and applied contributions has been tolerated as an imperfection, rather than a failure, of the measure itself.

Artificial intelligence has now made that tolerance untenable. The [*arXiv blog post*](https://blog.arxiv.org/2026/10/01/updated-rate-limit-policy/) by Kat Boboris on October 1st is a case in point. Much applied and technical work that once demanded effort can now be produced rapidly and at negligible cost. To get an idea of how out of control "paper inflation" has become, one only need note the number of arXiv submissions per month across all categories has doubled, from roughly 20,000 to 40,000, over the last two years. *It's particularly mind-numbing that some categories, for example cs.AI, have doubled in just the last nine months.*

A currency inflated beyond all reference to value ceases to function as one. The metric has not merely become imperfect; it has become outdated, obsolete, and perhaps even meaningless.

What should be done about this?

We do not propose abandoning publications, nor relinquishing the principle of making one's work public and accessible. On the contrary, we should move beyond treating "the paper" as a final product of a research program and instead towards dynamical contributions. For example, it could be standardized that researchers will be expected to maintain regularly updated repositories of accumulated and in-progress work.

Platforms such as **GitHub** have demonstrated, in the context of software, precisely the model we are proposing: version-controlled, publicly accessible repositories that record not merely the output but the entire process of its development. We suggest that the **arXiv**, as an established and trusted platform, is best positioned to extend its infrastructure in this direction. A **GitHub**-like environment, purpose-built for scientific research, would allow researchers to publish continuous updates to their work, with moderators able to inspect the repository at any point without requiring a formal submission cycle.

The unit of credit would no longer be the paper, but *the research line*, that is, the accumulated and documented development of an idea over time. An occasional public announcement in the form of an automated "repo progress report" could stand in as the modernized version of the paper.

Maintaining a live repository will allow a researcher to freely follow a problem at its own pace, to share intermediate results honestly without waiting for the neat closure a formal submission demands, and to let the work be seen as it grows. Our collective understanding would benefit too: a continuously updated body of work creates a living record that others can engage with, build upon, and be inspired by in real time, without waiting years for the formal literature to catch up. And over time, we believe, this is how a common scientific language will be built, one that crosses disciplines, that allows researchers in different fields to recognize in each other's work the questions they share.

The implications of such a change reach further than publishing shenanigans and number of publications. Currently, one way or another, every researcher feels the quiet weight of the annual review, the slow distortion that sets in when curiosity has to justify itself in paper counts, when the depth of a question matters less than whether it can be answered and submitted in time. That pressure does not merely cause discomfort; it shapes, over time, which questions we dare asking — we should not limit our questions and imagination, because then what is the point?

Shifting the incentives back towards exploring the higher-risk and novel approaches will allow us to look beyond our current self-imposed boundaries.
